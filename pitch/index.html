Edtech didn't fail here for lack of ideas. It shipped cloud bills, per-seat licenses and “requires fast internet” to barangays that have none — and it taught in a language the child doesn't think in.

Why not just ChatGPT?

A chatbot forgets your child.
This one remembers.

  • A chatbot gives the answer. A tutor must withhold it — one step, then ask the child for the next.
  • A child can't read or type. This is a spoken lesson, not a chat window.
  • ChatGPT doesn't speak Waray, Ilocano, Cebuano. It has no voice for the child to hear.
  • No one hands a first-grader an account. No login, no history, nickname only.
  • It runs where ChatGPT can't — a shared low-end phone, spotty data, priced for a public school.

The product

Speak it, or scan the page.

  • Voice-first. The child talks; the tutor answers in the mother tongue.
  • Their own worksheet. Scan a page → the tutor teaches that problem.
  • Code-switching. Reasoning in the home language, the formal term in Filipino/English.
  • One step per turn. Never the answer — it asks the child back.
  • Tap to answer. A number pad means it works in every language, even without speech recognition.

Lean by design

Free to the learner. Bounded to run.

  • No per-seat license, no account. A school pays nothing per child — and nothing for a child who never uses it.
  • Not “₱0 to run” — low and capped. Each new spoken step costs a little LLM and voice; the app records tokens, TTS characters and speech seconds for every session.
  • Cached audio is free across restarts. Every clip is keyed by a hash and stored, so repeats — greetings, “Balikan natin”, feedback — replay at ₱0 on any instance.
  • Caps are enforced server-side: 10 min/session · 30 min/week · 3 scans/session · 3 sessions/day.
  • We can show the number. The usage endpoint returns the real counters; set unit prices and it becomes a ₱ estimate per class — no slogan.
